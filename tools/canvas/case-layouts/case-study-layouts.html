Research

How do I get to know the problem better, more than just interviews and surveys

1. Conducting experimentation

Given an opportunity in a class where students have to collect their own data, I made my own mood visualization and realize this isn't an easy feat.

2. Second hand research from academic resource

From secondary resources, I gather more insights on what user want in a app, and the pro & con of different existing app resources.

3. Individual interviews

Compile findings

Discover patterns: compiling interviews and second-hand research paper insights to find commonalities

I gathered opinions from various sources during my research and compiled them into an affinity diagram to identify commonalities. Commonly mentioned pain points include timely entry and accurate mood representation.

Mapping

Painting the picture: who might need help, and what does their holistic journey look like?

Key findings

Comparing the overall user experience with a mood journal, it's clear that users place significant importance on the "Before" and "After" aspects. Many existing mood journals overlook these:

Before: the user's initial experience, incentives, and motivation to start using the mood journal.
After: the user's feelings immediately after using the journal and their wishes for improvements.

Reflect and refine: distilling core pain points and needs

Sensors

Thinking outside of the box: how can we push the role of the mood journal further

Inspired by a prototyping class, I explored the potential of sensors. While most mood journal apps use time-based reminders, time alone isn't a precise indicator for entries. Therefore, I conducted research focusing on: 1) reducing the effort required for entry creation, and 2) implementing low-touch interventions that can provide immediate mood improvement.

Research questions

Question 1: Can the mood logging experience be transformed from an active task into a passive one by utilizing physiological signals?
Question 2: Are there low-effort micro practices that can effectively alleviate stress and anxiety?

Key findings

For question 1: yes, physiological traits can indicate mood changes. Skin conductance, heart rate, and heart rate variability (HRV) all provide valuable insights into emotional states.
For question 2: micro practices that can help cope with negative moods include breathing exercises, HRV vibration, and fist clenching exercises.

Ideation

Story time: imagine a mood journal that goes beyond a simple entry log. It senses and communicates with you.

Most digital mood journals are mobile apps that require active logging in and out, which can be cumbersome. To address this, we could explore a different approach. Considering low-touch and sensing capabilities, an Apple Watch would be highly suitable.

Build notes

What the Mood.orb page ships today, and the options for turning one of the five layouts into the template every case study is built from. The Mood.orb / TikTok switch in the bar shows each layout with a long and a very short case.

Before anything else: the two folders are swapped

work/mood-orb/index.html contains the Hedra World case study, and work/hedraworld/index.html contains Mood.orb. The Work page links the Mood.orb card to the mood-orb folder, so clicking Mood.orb opens Hedra World today. The Webflow export kept the slugs crossed. Fix: swap the two data-href values on the Work page, or rename the folders when the pages are rebuilt.

02Template options

The Work page already has the pattern: one template, one build script, the site and the prototype from the same source. The case studies can join it three ways.

Option 1

Content file + layout template

Each case study becomes one file of content (content/mood-orb.html: the sections, headings, paragraphs and media list) and picks a layout by name. build.py renders it into work/mood-orb/index.html with the shared nav, scrims, fonts and ASCII footer injected.

You edit copy without touching layout, switch a case from A to B in one word, and the prototype artifact builds the same way it does for the Work page.

Option 2

Partials only

Keep each case page as a full HTML file, but pull the head, nav, footer and scripts from shared partials at build time. Least change to how the pages are written today; layout still lives in each file, so a change to the chapter rail is edited six times.

Option 3

Patch the Webflow export in place

Keep the current pages, strip the Webflow runtime, purge the CSS and re-export images. Fastest for one page, but every later change is manual and the six pages keep drifting from the Work page's type and spacing.

04Consistency with the Work page

  • Type. The case title uses the Work hero's scale and its two voices: grey PP Neue Montreal Medium with PP Editorial Old Italic accents in #545454. All three layouts above do this.
  • Nav and footer. The same gnav with the inverse-ink clone, the progressive scrims, and the pinned ASCII footer row. They come from the shared partials, so they cannot drift.
  • Surfaces. Media sits on the card grey (#ececec) with the 28–40px radius family; the showcase block is the same near-black (#161616) as the card's dark section today.
  • Pills. Section labels use the nav's pill, not the Webflow pink button, so Background / Challenge / Showcase read as the same family as Work / Craft / About.

05Motion

  • Entrance. Reuse the Work page's hero blur-in for the title and subtitle, and cardNear for the cover, with the same Orchestration defaults, so opening a case study feels like the grid opening.
  • Card to page. The Work page's openCard already fades a veil and shows the title; the case page can start with the cover in the card's position and expand, which needs only the card's rect passed in the URL hash.
  • Media reveal. Boards blur in from 8px as they enter the viewport (the spreads layout shows it); videos start on approach. All of it off under prefers-reduced-motion.
  • Return. Back to Work lands on the card you opened, using the returnHome setting that already exists.

06Reading and navigation

  • Body copy at 17px on a 65-character measure; boards break out to the full column width; captions at 12px in the subtitle grey.
  • A chapter rail (A), a pinned stage counter (B) or a dot rail with a counter (C) so a reader always knows how much is left. Each one uses the difference blend over dark sections, like the nav.
  • Numbered showcase rows, /01 to /04, in PP Editorial Old Italic, the same device as the Work page's accents.
  • A Next project card at the end of every case, so recruiters move through the portfolio without going back to the grid.
  • Head data per case: title, meta description, OG image from the cover; heading order h1 → h2 → h3.

07The two references

MetaLab, Atoms. Black page, one 140px light serif word for the title, a three-column meta row (label white, value grey), then a full-bleed hero video. Every chapter is a 20px title over a 56px statement that alternates between the left and right half of a 12-column grid, with 16px notes tucked beside the media. Media has no radius: full-bleed, 24px-gutter wide, or two 50/50 tiles. A marquee of paper cards, a centred 40px quote, stat rows with serif numerals over hairlines, and a giant "Fin. What's next?" behind the next project's phone. Whitespace between chapters is around 180px.

Daybreak Studio, Adaline brand. The case study opens as a white frame over the site, 5px inset with a hairline border and a close mark. One 14px uppercase eyebrow, one 48px line, then nothing but tiles: 1366px wide inside 37px gutters, 12px radius, 16px gaps, rows of one or two. There are no captions. A frosted pill at the bottom names the section you are on ("Brand Purpose +") and opens into a 400px card with a short paragraph and prev and next. It ends on a plain "Close".
